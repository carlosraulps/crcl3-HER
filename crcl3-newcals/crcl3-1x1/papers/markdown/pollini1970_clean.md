Introduction Chromium trihalides have recently received a good deal of attention because of their peculiar physical properties and possible applications [l to 51. These materials are insulators and become ferromagnetic below their Curie temperature. They have a typical layer structure [l] and unfilled d-shells, the presence of which determines the magnetic properties. Until recently [6] the studies of the intrinsic optical properties were limited to the so-called “crystal field l) A preliminary report of this work has been given a t the “International Symposium on Anisotropy in Layer Structures” Taormina, Italy, September 1968.

Various sharp peaks present in the paramagnetic [5] and ferromagnetic [l] phases and the main broad bands were attributed to the spin-forbidden quartet-to-doublet transitions and to the spin allowed quartet- to-quartet transitions, respectively. Experimental 2.2 Absorption and reflectivity All the visible and near ultraviolet (up to 6 eV) absorption spectra have been measured on a double beam recording spectro-photometer (type Cary 14). The specimens were mounted inside a stainless steel cryostat. Polarized ab- sorption data were obtained by inserting a properly mounted Rochon prism in the light path. Absorption coefficients of the order of lo4 to 105cm-l were measured for single crystals. A stripping technique in which self-adhesive tape was used to cleave the crystals provided us with samples of different thickness down to x 1 pm. The crystal was floated off its adhesive backing by immersion in chloroform and transferred onto a quartz support. Reflectivity data over the energy range 0.8 to 6 eV were obtained using a Cary 14 spectro-photometer, modified to work in reflection. Measurements were made using unpolarized light incident on the specimen surface at an angle of 25’ with respect to the c-axis of the crystal. Data beyond 6 eV were obtained with a vacuum uv Hilger grating monochromator; in this case the angle of incidence was 12.5’. Films were also grown inside the cryostat, and their thickness was continuously monitored by means of interference fringes obtained by reflection. The refractive index of the films, n, was measured by the well known inter- ference fringe technique which involves the determination of n at a fixed & together with the film thickness. This was done because the electrical conductivity in a direction perpendicular to the layers cannot be described with the standard concept of carrier mobilities. The fact that we find an Urbach’s rule behaviour at the first edge of CrC1, is a further confirmation of the general character of this rule, which occurs in such different situations as the intrinsic absorption edge in CdS and alkali halides, bound excitons in alkali halides, anthracene, and impurity states [9]. The presence of an Urbach’s rule at the low energy side of the 4T2 band is of considerable importance in our case since it should exclude the fact that the structure in the 4T2 may be due to phonons. The various Urbach’s rule explana- tions imply essentially a multi-phonon process [7, 101 and, therefore, absence of any vibrational structure. Further, if we choose an heuristic attitude, the exponential behaviour on the low energy side of the absorption edge increases the probability of observing the above mentioned structure, the absence of which has been demonstrated. To complete the argument we add that in general [1 11 the vibrational structures in solids are highly temperature depend- ent, but in this case no strong temperature variation was found. We now proceed to consider the two bands with their superimposed structures. The Laporte’s rule, forbidding transitions between levels of the same parity (in this case d-d levels), is invalidated because in this compound the chromium site is not an inversion centre. Most of the intensity of the band is then due to electric dipole transitions allowed by the mixing of odd parity states through the static crystalline field. Lattice vibrations will then broaden the bands and, through dynamical mixing, add some extra oscillator strength to the transitions. Such a contribution is rather small, relative to the one which lattice vibrations are able to give to forbidden transitions allowed essentially by the dynamical mixing. The strong temperature dependence of the oscillator strength [12] is typical of the second situation. We move now to detailed analysis of the spectrum of Fig. 2. Finally, through careful absorption measurements in the crystal field band region, we have found new structures. Summary New structures found in the absorption spectrum over the crystal field region together with optical anisotropy measurements, allowed us to propose different and more detailed assignments of the optical transitions involved. The photoconductivity induced in the same energy region may be due to electrons hopping between nearby Cr ions. With reference to the photoconductivity data at x 3.2 eV, possible explana- tions of the absorption edge in that energy region have been discussed. By means of absorption and reflectivity data several peaks in the 4 to 8 eV region have been observed. In order to clarify the role of the d levels in the band picture, and to obtain soundly based attributions of the peaks seen in the absorption and reflectivity spectra, more transport and optical property measurements on isostructural compounds are required.
